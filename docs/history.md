# The History of RUE

For me, this story starts in 2010 with the creation of Comme il Faut, a project I built alongside [Josh McCoy](https://joshmccoy.com), [Ben Samuel](http://www.ben-samuel.com), [Aaron Reed](https://aaronareed.net), and others while we were all pursuing PhDs as part of the [Expressive Intelligence Studio](https://eis.ucsc.edu) at the University of California, Santa Cruz. We were advised by [Michael Mateas](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~michaelm/) and [Noah Wardrip-Fruin](http://www.noahwf.com). The project itself has a rich intellectual history rooted in GOFAI (good old-fashioned AI) and story generation research, and we were deeply inspired by the work our amazing advisors.

The central idea behind [Comme il Faut (CiF)](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6732913) was to define a set of social norms that applied to every character in a simulation, creating what we called "social physics". Just as the physics in a game like Angry Birds enables surprising and emergent outcomes (blocks tumbling into other blocks, pigs going down in unexpected ways), our notion of social physics was meant to enable surprising and emergent social interactions between characters.

CiF was developed alongside the game [Prom Week](https://mtreanor.com/publications/promWeek-FDG2013.pdf), a social simulation game where the player chooses what high school characters say and do with one another in the week leading up to prom. The desires of individual characters, and how those characters responded to one another, were all determined by CiF. The system made use of over 5,000 influence rules that Ben and I wrote together, capturing what we thought of as "social common sense". These rules ranged from fairly straightforward logical assertions ("if you are my friend, I will be more nice to you") to more elaborate chains of social reasoning, like: "if I have a crush on you, and someone was mean to me, and you then were mean to that person, I might think 'you stood up for me and now I love you even more!'" Prom Week was nominated for Technical Excellence at the Independent Games Festival and was featured at several other events, including IndieCade, which was a real validation of the approach.

Around this same time, I created a game generation system called [Game-O-Matic](https://mtreanor.com/publications/Game-O-Matic-PCG2012.pdf) that used a similar approach to refine generated games through a generate/test/repair/test loop. Game-O-Matic wasn't a social simulation system, but it used CiF-like concepts to score the utility of various repair strategies: weighted rules evaluated against a state that represented a generated game's current design, which determined which modifications were appropriate to apply. In this case, the state was the current configuration of parameterized components in a custom component-based game engine. This is when I really started to understand that this weight rule approach had real benefits outside of social modeling. Social simulation remained a compelling use case, but the underlying mechanism was far more general than that.

CiF was later iterated upon and significantly enhanced by Ben and others at UC Santa Cruz into a system called [Ensemble](http://www.ben-samuel.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/FDG2015-The-Ensemble-Engine-Next-Generation-Social-Physics.pdf). Because CiF had been created alongside Prom Week, it was never as general or application-agnostic as we had hoped. Ensemble addressed this by introducing a schema for predicate types, a new and faster evaluation system, and several other improvements, ad some limitations.

Years later, in 2016, Josh and I, inspired by [Versu](https://versu.com), created a concept of social practices on top of Ensemble. [Social practices](https://mtreanor.com/publications/socialframework.pdf) are patterns of turn-taking social interaction in which each action is chosen by a CiF-like weighted-rule utility system navigating agents through a dialogue tree. We deployed this system with partners at the Educational Testing Service in a study of cross-cultural learning called The Ustradian Games. 

Some years later, driven by my desire to have a version of the "CiF approach" that wasn't only tied to social simulation, I created the "ScoredRuleEngine" (SRE). This was largely a hobby project of mine, but I did integrate it into a prototype for an evolution of Game-O-Matic I called "The Game-O-Mat". One feature in this system was that it was able to compute dynamic predicates based on external code (i.e. sensors). This was useful because I had it running in realtime alongside 2d arcade-style games, recognizing emergent gameplay patterns. This was never finalized, but it informed what was to come.

In 2024, I started working on social practices again. As had happened with CiF before it, the Ustradian Games had accumulated enough application-specific cruft that the Ensemble/Social Practices codebase had become genuinely difficult to work with. The authoring experience was also extremely clunky (our tool was a mess). In what I can only immodestly describe as a herculean effort, I cleaned up the whole system and built a suite of tools to make it usable: a cloud-based spreadsheet DSL for editing and sharing social state and influence rules, and a robust social practice authoring tool. This system, built together with Ben, is called ESP (Ensemble with Social Practices), and [it is ready for anyone to use](https://github.com/mtreanor/esp).

Ben and I also used ESP to create a vertical slice demo of a game called [Slice of Life](https://mtreanor.com/publications/fdg2025-sliceOfLife.pdf), a Christmas Carol-themed game about a pizzeria chef revisiting his past to rediscover his passion for pizza and for the people around him. That project is sadly on hold, but [you can read about it here](https://mtreanor.com/publications/fdg2025-sliceOfLife.pdf).

Which brings us to RUE. Across this 16-year period, and really ever since I first understood logic programming, I have wanted a robust, expressive, and feature-rich authoring system that worked precisely the way I needed it to. RUE is my attempt to overcome every challenge I faced while creating and using CiF, Ensemble, Game-O-Matic (and [its less successful successor Game-O-Mat](https://mtreanor.com/publications/treanor_gameomat_engine.pdf)), and ESP (though [ESP is pretty great; check it out!](https://github.com/mtreanor/esp)).

In all this time, the most interesting thing to me about this work has been when simulation output is surprising but reasonable. The system makes unexpected, complicated decisions based on state larger than any one person could hold in their head, and when you look into it, the reasoning, while perhaps not what you would have guessed, is totally sound. I knew that this CiF-like approach could produce that kind of output, but the "reasonable" part was always something you had to partially reconstruct yourself. I always want to know why things were true! So RUE was developed from the ground up with provenance as a first-class feature. Every fact can be traced back to the specific rules, bindings, or actions that caused it, so you can build a full explanation tree on demand rather than reverse-engineering one after the fact.

You might reasonably ask: why not just use Datalog? Did you know ASP can do 100x more than RUE? Why did you violate such-and-such property that would guarantee whatever? The answer is that I just don't work well unless I do things from scratch. So if I have meaninglessly reinvented the wheel, that's just how it's gotta be.
